\begin{abstract}
\noindent
The Calango Native DFT Engine (\file{src/dft}) is an all-electron Kohn--Sham
solver written directly in C++, using numerical atomic orbitals as its basis.
It treats every electron explicitly --- no pseudopotential and no frozen core
--- and runs inside the application rather than by generating a script for an
external program.

This document gives the mathematical formulation of the engine: the basis and
its generation, the multicentre integration grids, the construction of the
overlap and Hamiltonian matrices, the exchange--correlation functionals,
Brillouin-zone sampling, the self-consistency loop, and the energy
derivatives.

\textbf{Every section is badged with its implementation status.} The ground
state is complete and validated; gradient functionals, symmetry reduction of
the $k$-point set, forces and stress are formulated here as
\emph{specification} for work not yet done. Section~\ref{sec:validation}
gives the numbers the implemented parts are checked against, and states
plainly which quantities have no external reference.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\implemented

\subsection{What the engine is}

Two choices define it, and nearly every design decision follows from one of
them.

\textbf{All-electron.} Every electron is carried explicitly. This removes the
pseudopotential from the list of things a result depends on: a number is wrong
only because the functional or the basis is, not because of a transferability
assumption made by whoever generated the potential. It is also what makes
core-level properties computable at all.

\textbf{Numerical atomic orbitals (NAO).} Basis functions are tabulated on a
radial mesh rather than expanded in analytic primitives, so a basis function
can be the \emph{exact numerical solution} of the atomic problem. The free
atom is therefore essentially exact at the smallest possible basis size, and
each further level has a physical meaning. Confinement makes each function
\emph{exactly} zero beyond a finite radius, which is what turns the overlap
and Hamiltonian matrices sparse.

The price is paid in the integrals: nothing is analytic. Every matrix element
is a numerical quadrature over a real-space grid, so \emph{the grid is the
accuracy}, and most of the engineering lives in building grids that are dense
where the wavefunction has structure and sparse where it does not.

\subsection{Units and conventions}

Hartree atomic units throughout ($\hbar = m_e = e = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 = 1$);
lengths in bohr, energies in hartree. The application boundary converts to
{\AA}ngstr\"om and electronvolt, once. Spherical harmonics are the
\emph{real} $Y_{\ell m}$, normalised so $\int |Y_{\ell m}|^2 \dd\Omega = 1$,
without the Condon--Shortley phase.

\section{Basis sets}
\label{sec:basis}
\implemented

\subsection{Form of a numerical atomic orbital}

A basis function centred on nucleus $A$ is a tabulated radial function times a
real spherical harmonic,
\begin{equation}
  \phi_{A i}(\vb{r}) \;=\; \frac{u_{n\ell}(r_A)}{r_A}\,
                            Y_{\ell m}(\hat{\vb{r}}_A),
  \qquad \vb{r}_A = \vb{r} - \vb{R}_A .
  \label{eq:nao}
\end{equation}
The radial part is stored as $u = rR$ rather than $R$. That is not a
bookkeeping detail: $u$ is what the radial Schr\"odinger equation is written
in,
\begin{equation}
  u'' \;=\; \left[ \frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^2} + 2\bigl(v(r) - \varepsilon\bigr)
            \right] u ,
  \label{eq:radial}
\end{equation}
with no first-derivative term; $u$ vanishes at the origin for every $\ell$, so
the nuclear cusp is not a numerical problem; and $\int_0^\infty |u|^2 \dd r =
1$ is the normalisation, with no $r^2$ weight to lose precision on.

\subsection{The logarithmic radial mesh}

Every all-electron quantity is dominated by a region a fraction of a bohr
across. The $1s$ density of a third-row element lives inside $0.1\,a_0$ while
the valence tail runs to ten. The mesh is therefore
\begin{equation}
  r(i) \;=\; a\left(e^{b i} - 1\right), \qquad i = 0,\dots,N-1 ,
  \label{eq:mesh}
\end{equation}
which starts \emph{exactly} at $r=0$ --- so the cusp sits on a grid point
rather than between two --- and turns every radial integral into a uniformly
spaced one in $i$:
\begin{equation}
  \int f(r)\dd r \;=\; \int f\bigl(r(i)\bigr)\,\dv{r}{i}\dd i ,
  \qquad \dv{r}{i} = b\,(r + a).
\end{equation}
Simpson's rule applies directly in $i$. Two consequences are used
throughout: interpolation is done in the \emph{index}, where the mesh is
uniform and a cubic is well conditioned everywhere, and the index for a given
radius is obtained by inverting~\eqref{eq:mesh} in closed form,
$i = \ln(1 + r/a)/b$, rather than by searching.

Running integrals use \emph{cumulative} Simpson, not partial sums of the
composite weights: the $(1,4,2,\dots,4,1)/3$ pattern is a valid quadrature
only for the whole interval, and truncating it leaves a $4$ or a $2$ where an
endpoint $1$ belongs --- an $\order{h}$ error at every point instead of
$\order{h^4}$.

\subsection{The free atom}

Basis generation starts by solving the spherical, non-spin-polarised free atom
self-consistently. Equation~\eqref{eq:radial} is integrated as a first-order
pair $(u, \dv*{u}{r})$ in the mesh index by classical Runge--Kutta.

The eigenvalue is found by \textbf{bisection on the node count}. The number of
nodes of the outward solution is a non-decreasing step function of
$\varepsilon$ that jumps by one at each eigenvalue, so bracketing the jump
brackets the eigenvalue --- with no derivative, no matching condition, and no
way to converge to a neighbouring state however close it lies. The
wavefunction is then rebuilt by joining an outward solution to an inward one
at the classical turning point, because beyond that point the outward solution
is the \emph{growing} one and what it actually carries is rounding error
amplified exponentially.

\subsection{Confinement}
\label{sec:confinement}

Strict locality requires each function to vanish identically beyond a cutoff.
This is imposed as a \emph{finite potential barrier added to the potential the
orbital is solved in}, of the form of Junquera \emph{et al.}~\cite{junquera}:
\begin{equation}
  v_{\text{conf}}(r) \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    0, & r \le r_i ,\\[4pt]
    \displaystyle
    V_0\,\frac{\exp\!\left[-\,(r_c - r_i)/(r - r_i)\right]}{r_c - r},
      & r_i < r < r_c ,\\[10pt]
    \infty, & r \ge r_c ,
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:confine}
\end{equation}
with onset $r_i = r_c - w$. It is zero inside the onset, and every derivative
is continuous where it switches on. In practice the barrier is capped at a
finite height, because the radial integrator takes a fixed step in the mesh
index and an uncapped barrier drives $\sqrt{2v}\,\Delta r$ outside the
stability region of the integrator.

\paragraph{Why a barrier and not a wall.}
This is worth stating precisely, because the obvious alternative --- solving
the atom in a box, $u(r_c) = 0$ --- is wrong in a way that is almost
invisible. A hard wall gives $u(r_c) = 0$ with $u'(r_c) \neq 0$. The orbital
is then continuous at the cutoff sphere while its gradient jumps, so
$\laplacian\phi$ contains a \emph{surface delta function} on that sphere. The
engine stores the kinetic operator per function as
$(\varepsilon - v)u$ (Section~\ref{sec:kinetic}), which is built from the
smooth part of $u''$ alone and cannot represent that delta.

What that costs is hidden everywhere except one place. Diagonal elements are
untouched, because the delta is weighted by $u(r_c) = 0$ --- so
self-consistency, electron counts and kinetic-energy identities all still
pass. Every \emph{inter-atomic} element is short by a surface term, because a
neighbour's orbital is not zero on this atom's cutoff sphere. The observable
signature is exactly that: isolated atoms exact, two atoms far apart exact,
and an error that grows with coordination and with the number of cutoff
spheres in the basis. Table~\ref{tab:wall} in Section~\ref{sec:validation}
gives the measured size.

A finite barrier removes the delta instead of approximating it: $v$ stays
finite everywhere, so $-\tfrac12\laplacian\phi = (\varepsilon - v)\phi$ holds
at \emph{every} radius, and $u$ reaches the cutoff with both it and its
derivative already damped away.

\subsection{Basis tiers}
\label{sec:tiers}

The basis is built in nested levels, each strictly containing the last.

\paragraph{Tier 1 (minimal).} The confined atom's own occupied orbitals ---
all of them, core included, because this is an all-electron method and no
pseudopotential stands in for the core.

\paragraph{Tier 2 (double zeta plus polarisation).} A second radial function
for each \emph{valence} angular momentum, plus one shell at the first $\ell$
the free atom does not occupy ($d$ for silicon). The polarisation function is
a genuine solution of~\eqref{eq:radial} in the confined atom's potential:
unbound in the free atom, bound in the barrier.

The second zeta is the \emph{split-valence} construction, which needs no
bonded reference calculation and no fitting. A radius $r_s$ is chosen so that
a fixed fraction of the orbital's norm lies beyond it; inside $r_s$ the
orbital is replaced by a smooth polynomial, and the new basis function is the
difference:
\begin{equation}
  u^{(2\zeta)}(r) \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    u(r) - r^{\ell+1}\bigl(a - b r^2 + c r^4\bigr), & r < r_s,\\[4pt]
    0, & r \ge r_s .
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:split}
\end{equation}
The three coefficients match the orbital in \textbf{value, slope and
curvature} at $r_s$. Two parameters --- matching value and slope only --- is
the form usually quoted, and it is not sufficient here for the same reason a
hard wall is not: $-\tfrac12\laplacian\phi$ then jumps at $r_s$, and a
multicentre grid with a few tens of radial shells cannot integrate a
discontinuity. Matching the curvature makes $u$, $u'$ and $u''$ all vanish at
$r_s$, so the kinetic operator goes smoothly to zero.

The kinetic energy of the added function stays analytic. For
$F = r^\ell(a - br^2 + cr^4)$ the $r^{\ell-2}$ terms of the Laplacian cancel
against the centrifugal term exactly, leaving
\begin{equation}
  -\tfrac12 \laplacian \bigl[F(r) Y_{\ell m}\bigr]
  \;=\; \Bigl[ b(2\ell+3) - 2c(2\ell+5)r^2 \Bigr] r^{\ell}\, Y_{\ell m}.
  \label{eq:splitkinetic}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Tier 3.} A third zeta for the valence channels and a second zeta on
the polarisation channel.

\section{Integration grids}
\label{sec:grids}
\implemented

There is no analytic integral anywhere in the engine, so the grid \emph{is}
the accuracy of every number it produces. Its construction answers one
question: how do you integrate a function with a cusp at every nucleus and
smooth behaviour in between, without a mesh fine enough for the cusps
everywhere?

\subsection{Multicentre partition}

Becke's answer~\cite{becke}, which the engine implements: give every atom its
own spherical grid, dense at its own nucleus, and multiply each by a smooth
weight $w_A(\vb{r})$ with
\begin{equation}
  \sum_A w_A(\vb{r}) = 1 \ \ \text{everywhere}, \qquad
  w_A \approx 1 \ \text{near } \vb{R}_A , \quad
  w_A \approx 0 \ \text{near any other nucleus}.
\end{equation}
Then $\int f = \sum_A \int w_A f$, and each term integrates a function that is
smooth except at \emph{its own} centre --- exactly what a spherical grid is
good at. The weight is built from the elliptical coordinate between each pair
of centres,
\begin{equation}
  \mu_{AB} = \frac{r_A - r_B}{R_{AB}},
\end{equation}
smoothed by $p(x) = \tfrac12(3x - x^3)$ iterated three times,
\begin{equation}
  s(\mu) = \tfrac12\Bigl[1 - p\bigl(p(p(\mu))\bigr)\Bigr],
  \qquad
  w_A = \frac{\prod_{B \neq A} s(\nu_{AB})}
             {\sum_C \prod_{B \neq C} s(\nu_{CB})} ,
\end{equation}
where $\nu$ is $\mu$ shifted by the atoms' Bragg--Slater radii so the boundary
between a large atom and a small one sits where the density says it should.

For a periodic system the partition must see the atoms' \emph{periodic
images}, or the weights stop summing to one at the cell boundary and the
integrated electron count comes out short by whatever charge lives there.

\subsection{Radial and angular rules}

Radial shells use Gauss--Chebyshev quadrature of the second kind under the
map $r = R(1+x)/(1-x)$, which sends a finite interval to the half line, so a
fixed number of nodes covers the cusp and the tail at once.

Angular points use Lebedev rules~\cite{lebedev} --- the octahedrally symmetric
optimum, exact for all spherical harmonics up to a given degree with roughly
half the points a product rule needs --- with a generated
Gauss--Legendre $\times$ uniform-$\varphi$ product rule above the tabulated
orders. Both are verified rather than trusted: the test suite integrates
$\int Y_{\ell m} Y_{\ell' m'} \dd\Omega$ on each rule and requires
$\delta_{\ell\ell'}\delta_{mm'}$ to machine precision. A mistyped Lebedev
weight is otherwise completely silent.

\paragraph{Angular resolution is set by the partition, not the density.}
The Becke weight has a sharp boundary between atoms, and it is harder to
integrate than anything else on the grid. Integrating unity over a silicon
primitive cell --- which must return the cell volume --- is in error by
$10\%$ at degree 11 and reaches $5\times10^{-5}$ only at degree 29.

\section{Hamiltonian and matrix assembly}
\label{sec:hamiltonian}
\implemented

With Bloch sums $\chi_i^{\vb{k}}$ defined in Section~\ref{sec:bz}, every
matrix element is a quadrature over the multicentre grid:
\begin{align}
  S_{ij}(\vb{k}) &= \sum_g w_g\, \chi_i^{\vb{k}*}(\vb{r}_g)\,
                                  \chi_j^{\vb{k}}(\vb{r}_g), \\
  H_{ij}(\vb{k}) &= \sum_g w_g\, \chi_i^{\vb{k}*}(\vb{r}_g)
                    \Bigl[ \hat{T} + v_{\text{eff}}(\vb{r}_g) \Bigr]
                    \chi_j^{\vb{k}}(\vb{r}_g).
\end{align}

\subsection{Kinetic energy}
\label{sec:kinetic}

The kinetic term is \textbf{never differenced}. Each basis function solves the
radial equation of the confined atom it came from, so
\begin{equation}
  -\tfrac12 \laplacian \phi_i \;=\; \bigl(\varepsilon_i - v_{\text{at}}(r)\bigr)\phi_i
  \label{eq:kinid}
\end{equation}
holds identically, and $\hat{T}$ acting on a basis function is a
\emph{multiplication} by a tabulated function. Written the obvious way --- a
numerical Laplacian, or the symmetric $\grad\phi_i \cdot \grad\phi_j$ form ---
the largest single term in the total energy would carry the noise of one or
two numerical derivatives of an interpolated table.

Equation~\eqref{eq:kinid} requires $v_{\text{at}}$ to be the \emph{full}
potential the orbital solves, confining barrier included
(Section~\ref{sec:confinement}). Each function therefore carries its own
tabulated array
\begin{equation}
  t_i(r) \;\equiv\; r \cdot \bigl(-\tfrac12\laplacian\phi_i\bigr)\big/ Y_{\ell m}
         \;=\; \bigl(\varepsilon_i - v_{\text{at}}(r)\bigr) u_i(r),
\end{equation}
which is smooth through the origin (it behaves as $Z r^\ell$, the $1/r$ of the
nucleus being cancelled by the $r^{\ell+1}$ of $u$). Carrying the array rather
than the eigenvalue is what allows basis functions that are \emph{not}
eigenstates of anything --- a split-valence zeta is an orbital minus a
polynomial, and its kinetic array comes from~\eqref{eq:splitkinetic}. The
identity also inverts,
\begin{equation}
  u'' = \frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^2}u - 2 t ,
\end{equation}
so a function's curvature is available without any numerical second
derivative.

The matrix is symmetrised,
$\tfrac12\bigl[\langle\phi_i|\hat{T}\phi_j\rangle +
\langle\hat{T}\phi_i|\phi_j\rangle\bigr]$, because the identity holds with
each function's own $\varepsilon$ and $v_{\text{at}}$.

\subsection{Electrostatics}
\label{sec:electrostatics}

The total charge of the system --- electrons plus nuclei --- is written as a
sum of \emph{neutral} free atoms plus a difference density:
\begin{equation}
  n(\vb{r}) \;=\; \sum_{A,\vb{T}}
     \Bigl[\rho_A^{\text{free}}(\vb{r}-\vb{R}_A-\vb{T})
           - Z_A\,\delta(\vb{r}-\vb{R}_A-\vb{T})\Bigr]
     \;+\; \delta\rho(\vb{r}) .
  \label{eq:na}
\end{equation}
Each bracket is neutral, so its potential decays \emph{exponentially} instead
of as $1/r$ and the lattice sum converges absolutely --- no Ewald summation,
no conditional convergence, no compensating background. The neutral-atom
potential is
\begin{equation}
  v_A^{\text{NA}}(r) \;=\; -\frac{Z_A}{r} + v_H\bigl[\rho_A^{\text{free}}\bigr](r).
\end{equation}
In the engine the nuclear $1/r$ is subtracted analytically at each point and
only the screened remainder is interpolated, because interpolating
$v^{\text{NA}}$ itself means fitting a cubic across a pole at the one place
the density is largest.

What is left, $\delta\rho$, is the small bonding rearrangement, and it
integrates to zero over the cell. Its potential is solved centre by centre.
The grid points of atom $A$ already carry the Becke weight, so they \emph{are}
$w_A \delta\rho$ sampled on a spherical grid; expanding in real spherical
harmonics,
\begin{equation}
  c_{\ell m}^A(r) = \int \delta\rho_A(r,\Omega)\, Y_{\ell m}(\Omega)\dd\Omega ,
\end{equation}
the radial Poisson equation has the closed solution
\begin{equation}
  V_{\ell m}^A(r) \;=\; \frac{4\pi}{2\ell+1}
    \left[ \frac{1}{r^{\ell+1}}\int_0^r c_{\ell m}^A(r')\, r'^{\,\ell+2}\dd r'
         + r^{\ell}\int_r^{\infty} c_{\ell m}^A(r')\, r'^{\,1-\ell}\dd r'
    \right],
  \label{eq:poisson}
\end{equation}
continued outside the sphere by its exact multipole tail. The total
electrostatic potential and energy are then
\begin{align}
  v_{\text{es}}(\vb{r}) &= \sum_{A,\vb{T}} v_A^{\text{NA}}
        \bigl(\abs{\vb{r}-\vb{R}_A-\vb{T}}\bigr) + v_H[\delta\rho](\vb{r}),\\
  E_{\text{es}} &= E_{\text{ref}}
        + \int \delta\rho\, \sum_{A,\vb{T}} v_A^{\text{NA}}
        + \tfrac12 \int \delta\rho\; v_H[\delta\rho] ,
\end{align}
where $E_{\text{ref}}$ --- each free atom's own electrostatic self-energy plus
every neutral-atom pair interaction --- depends only on the geometry and is
computed once. Note that $v_{\text{es}} = \delta E_{\text{es}}/\delta\rho$
exactly, which is what keeps the energy and the Hamiltonian consistent.

\paragraph{Known limitation.} The monopole tail of $\delta\rho$ is truncated.
That is exact when each atom's difference density is neutral, which symmetry
guarantees for a crystal whose atoms are all equivalent, and an approximation
otherwise. The engine computes the per-atom monopole regardless and reports it
when it is not negligible, rather than absorbing it silently.

\section{Exchange--correlation functionals}
\label{sec:xc}

\subsection{Local density approximation}
\label{sec:lda}
\implemented

Exchange is Dirac--Slater, which is not a parameterisation of anything --- it
is the exact exchange energy density of the uniform electron gas:
\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon_x(\rho) = -\frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{3\rho}{\pi}\right)^{1/3},
  \qquad
  v_x = \pdv{(\rho\varepsilon_x)}{\rho} = \frac{4}{3}\varepsilon_x .
\end{equation}
That factor of $4/3$ is exact --- $\rho\varepsilon_x$ scales as $\rho^{4/3}$
--- and is the cheapest available check that an exchange implementation is
right.

Three correlation parameterisations of the same Ceperley--Alder quantum Monte
Carlo electron gas are available, written in
$r_s = (3/4\pi\rho)^{1/3}$: Perdew--Zunger~\cite{pz}, Vosko--Wilk--Nusair
(formula V)~\cite{vwn}, and Perdew--Wang 1992~\cite{pw92}, which is the
default. PW92 is the default for a practical reason: it is what most
plane-wave codes mean by ``LDA'' without qualification, so a number computed
with it can be compared against theirs to every digit the grids support,
rather than to the few meV per electron by which the fits differ. Its form is
a single analytic expression over the whole density range,
\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon_c(r_s) = -2A(1+\alpha_1 r_s)\,
    \ln\!\left[1 + \frac{1}{2A\bigl(\beta_1 r_s^{1/2} + \beta_2 r_s
      + \beta_3 r_s^{3/2} + \beta_4 r_s^{2}\bigr)}\right],
\end{equation}
with the potential following from
$v_c = \varepsilon_c - \tfrac{r_s}{3}\,\dv*{\varepsilon_c}{r_s}$, taken
analytically and verified against a finite difference of $\rho\varepsilon_c$
in the test suite.

Energy and potential are computed and returned \emph{separately}. Computing
the energy as $\int\rho\,v_{xc}$ --- a mistake that survives an SCF, because
the density still converges --- overestimates the exchange energy by exactly
one third.

\subsection{Generalised gradient approximation (PBE)}
\label{sec:pbe}
\implemented

A GGA is written in the density and the square of its gradient,
$\sigma = \abs{\grad\rho}^2$, as an energy per unit \emph{volume}
$f(\rho,\sigma)$ --- there is no natural per-electron split once a gradient
term is present. The engine returns $f$, $\partial f/\partial\rho$ and
$\partial f/\partial\sigma$ from one interface for both families; an LDA
simply has $\partial f/\partial\sigma \equiv 0$, so the assembler needs one
code path.

PBE exchange multiplies the uniform-gas energy density by
\begin{equation}
  F_x(s) = 1 + \kappa - \frac{\kappa}{1 + \mu s^2/\kappa},
  \qquad
  s = \frac{\abs{\grad\rho}}{2 k_F \rho},
  \quad k_F = (3\pi^2\rho)^{1/3},
\end{equation}
with $\kappa = 0.804$ fixed by the Lieb--Oxford bound and $\mu = \beta\pi^2/3$
chosen to recover the gradient expansion of the linear \emph{response}.
Everything is implemented in $t = s^2$ rather than $s$, so no square root of
$\sigma$ appears: the derivatives stay rational and $\sigma = 0$ is an
ordinary point rather than one where $\partial s/\partial\sigma$ diverges.

Correlation adds to the PW92 local part the gradient term
\begin{equation}
  H = \gamma \ln\!\left[ 1 + \frac{\beta}{\gamma}\,
      \frac{T(1 + AT)}{1 + AT + A^2T^2} \right],
  \qquad
  A = \frac{\beta/\gamma}{e^{-\varepsilon_c^{\text{unif}}/\gamma} - 1},
\end{equation}
with $T = t^2$, $t = \abs{\grad\rho}/(2k_s\rho)$ and $k_s = \sqrt{4k_F/\pi}$.
Note that $A$ depends on the density \emph{through} $\varepsilon_c$, so
$\partial f/\partial\rho$ carries a term through $A$ that is easy to omit; it
is included, and the derivative is confirmed against a finite difference of
the energy rather than trusted. As $\varepsilon_c \to 0^-$ in the low-density
tail $A$ diverges while $H \to 0$; the limit is taken explicitly instead of
passing through an intermediate infinity.

\subsubsection{Density gradients}

$\grad\rho$ is analytic, not differenced. With the density matrix $D_{ij}$,
\begin{equation}
  \rho = \sum_{ij} D_{ij}\phi_i\phi_j,
  \qquad
  \grad\rho = 2\sum_{ij} D_{ij}\,\phi_i\,\grad\phi_j,
\end{equation}
and the gradient of a single orbital follows from writing it as
$\phi = g(r)\,S_{\ell m}$ with $S_{\ell m} = r^{\ell}Y_{\ell m}$ the real
\emph{solid} harmonic --- a homogeneous polynomial:
\begin{equation}
  \grad\phi = \left( R' - \frac{\ell R}{r} \right) \hat{\vb{r}}\, Y_{\ell m}
            + \frac{R}{r^{\ell}}\,\grad S_{\ell m}.
  \label{eq:gradphi}
\end{equation}
Both terms are finite everywhere. The obvious alternative --- differentiating
$Y_{\ell m}$ in spherical angles --- carries a $1/\sin\theta$ and is singular
at the poles.

The radial derivative $\dv*{u}{r}$ is tabulated once per basis function with a
\emph{five}-point stencil in the mesh index. The three-point form is not
enough: a split-valence function is already the difference of two similar
quantities and loses digits to cancellation before any stencil error is added,
which left $\grad\phi$ about a percent off for exactly those functions while
the ordinary orbitals were correct to a few parts in ten thousand.

\subsubsection{The matrix element}

The GGA potential contains $\div(\partial f/\partial\grad\rho)$, and taking a
divergence numerically on an unstructured multicentre grid is both awkward and
noisy. The matrix element is therefore integrated by parts:
\begin{equation}
  \mel{\phi_i}{v_{xc}}{\phi_j} =
    \int \pdv{f}{\rho}\,\phi_i^{*}\phi_j \dd\vb{r}
    + \int \vb{V}\cdot\left( \phi_i^{*}\grad\phi_j
                           + \phi_j\grad\phi_i^{*} \right) \dd\vb{r},
  \qquad
  \vb{V} = 2\pdv{f}{\sigma}\grad\rho .
  \label{eq:ggamatrix}
\end{equation}
This needs only $\grad\phi$ --- no second derivatives, no divergence of a
sampled vector field --- and is manifestly Hermitian.

One consequence propagates into the energy. The kinetic energy can no longer
be obtained as the band energy minus a potential trace, because that form
assumes $v_{\text{eff}}$ is a local multiplier and the second term
of~\eqref{eq:ggamatrix} is not. $T_s$ is instead taken directly as the
expectation value of $\hat{T}$ over the occupied orbitals, which is exact for
both families and reproduces the local result unchanged.

\subsubsection{Validation}

Checked in three independent ways.

\emph{Against arithmetic}: $\partial f/\partial\rho$ and
$\partial f/\partial\sigma$ agree with finite differences of $f$ to
$2\times10^{-10}$ across five decades of density and gradient, and at
$\sigma = 0$ PBE reduces to LDA-PW92 to $10^{-16}$ --- exactly, as its
construction requires. Equation~\eqref{eq:gradphi} agrees with a finite
difference of $\phi$ to $4\times10^{-4}$ for every function of a
double-zeta-plus-polarisation basis.

\emph{Against another implementation}: $f$ and $\partial f/\partial\rho$ agree
with an independent PBE kernel to $10^{-6}$ relative.

\emph{Against physics}: the whole pipeline shifts the silicon atom's total
energy by $-27.37$~eV on going from LDA to PBE, against $-27.46$~eV from an
independent all-electron atomic solver --- the $0.09$~eV residue being the
confinement, not the functional. The cohesive energy of bulk silicon moves
from $5.68$ to $5.21$~eV/atom, which is the direction and roughly the size of
the known reduction of LDA's overbinding (published values are near $5.3$ and
$4.6$, with experiment at $4.63$).

\paragraph{Limitation.} The radial atomic solver is LDA-only, so the basis
functions and the free-atom reference density of a PBE run are generated from
an LDA-PW92 atom. That is ordinary practice --- the basis is a variational
space, and the crystal is solved self-consistently in the functional actually
requested --- and the engine states it in the run log rather than leaving it
to be discovered.

\section{Brillouin-zone integration}
\label{sec:bz}

\subsection{Bloch sums and the generalised eigenproblem}
\implemented

For a periodic system the basis functions are Bloch-summed over lattice
translations,
\begin{equation}
  \chi_i^{\vb{k}}(\vb{r}) \;=\; \sum_{\vb{T}}
     e^{i\vb{k}\cdot\vb{T}}\; \phi_i(\vb{r} - \bm{\tau}_i - \vb{T}).
  \label{eq:bloch}
\end{equation}
The sum over $\vb{T}$ is \emph{finite}, not truncated: $\phi$ is exactly zero
beyond its confinement radius. With $\vb{k}$ in fractional coordinates and
$\vb{T}$ in integer lattice multiples the phase is simply
$e^{2\pi i\,(\vb{k}\cdot\vb{n})}$, which removes the commonest sign-and-factor
error in a $k$-point loop.

The resulting problem is the complex Hermitian generalised eigenproblem
\begin{equation}
  H(\vb{k})\,C(\vb{k}) \;=\; S(\vb{k})\,C(\vb{k})\,E(\vb{k}).
\end{equation}
It is solved by \textbf{canonical orthogonalisation}, not by the Cholesky
reduction that a standard routine would use, and the difference matters for
this basis. Atomic orbitals on nearby atoms are not linearly independent:
squeeze two atoms together, or add diffuse functions, and $S$ acquires
eigenvalues at the $10^{-6}$ level and below. Cholesky of such an $S$
factorises a numerically singular matrix and the resulting orbitals are noise
amplified by $1/\sqrt{s}$. Canonical orthogonalisation instead diagonalises
$S = U s U^{\dagger}$, \emph{discards} the directions with
$s < \eta\, s_{\max}$, and works in the surviving subspace:
\begin{equation}
  X = U s^{-1/2}\big|_{\text{kept}},
  \qquad
  H' = X^{\dagger} H X,
  \qquad
  C = X C' .
\end{equation}
The number of discarded directions is reported, because a nonzero count is a
basis problem rather than an eigensolver problem and this is the only place
that can say so.

A Hermitian $A + iB$ with $A$ symmetric and $B$ antisymmetric is solved
through the real embedding
$\begin{psmallmatrix} A & -B \\ B & A \end{psmallmatrix}$,
whose spectrum is that of the Hermitian matrix with each eigenvalue twice.
This costs a factor of eight in a step that is not the bottleneck and avoids a
separate complex eigensolver to write, test, and get wrong.

\subsection{Monkhorst--Pack meshes}
\implemented

The sampling is a $\Gamma$-centred Monkhorst--Pack mesh~\cite{mp},
\begin{equation}
  \vb{k}_{pqr} = \frac{p}{N_1}\vb{b}_1 + \frac{q}{N_2}\vb{b}_2
               + \frac{r}{N_3}\vb{b}_3,
\end{equation}
with uniform weights $1/(N_1N_2N_3)$ summing to one. For a finite system the
only $k$-point is $\Gamma$, and that is not a choice.

Occupations come from a Fermi--Dirac distribution with the chemical potential
found by bisection on the electron count. This is not a convenience for
metals: it is required for convergence at all. An open shell --- silicon's
$3p^2$ is two electrons in a threefold degenerate level --- has arbitrary
eigenvectors within the degenerate subspace, and filling states one at a time
hands both electrons to whichever vector the eigensolver returned first. That
choice rotates every iteration: the energy stands still to eight digits while
the density swings by more than an electron, indefinitely. A Fermi
distribution gives equal occupation to equal eigenvalues by construction.

\subsection{Irreducible-zone reduction}
\label{sec:ibz}
\implemented

Symmetry-equivalent $k$-points are found and computed once. The algorithm is
exact integer arithmetic throughout, which means orbit membership is decided
by comparison and not by a tolerance:

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item The \textbf{lattice} point group: every integer matrix $W$ with
        $W^{\!\top} G W = G$, $G_{ij} = \vb{a}_i\cdot\vb{a}_j$ the metric.
        That condition says precisely that the corresponding Cartesian map is
        orthogonal \emph{and} takes the lattice to itself. Candidates are
        generated from the observation that a symmetry sends each lattice
        vector to a lattice vector of the same length, so the search is a
        product of three short lists rather than a sweep over integer
        matrices. The images are the \textbf{columns} of $W$: with
        $\vb{p}' = W\vb{p}$ on fractional coordinates, $\vb{a}_j$ has
        coordinates $\vb{e}_j$ and its image is $W\vb{e}_j$.
  \item The \textbf{crystal} point group: those $W$ for which some translation
        $\vb{t}$ makes $\vb{p} \to W\vb{p} + \vb{t}$ map the atoms onto
        themselves, species by species. Candidate translations are complete
        and few, since any valid one must carry the first atom onto some atom
        of its own species.
  \item In reciprocal space $\vb{k} \to W^{-\top}\vb{k}$, which follows from
        requiring $\vb{k}\cdot\vb{p}$ invariant, and is again an integer
        matrix because $\det W = \pm 1$.
  \item Time reversal adds $\vb{k}\to-\vb{k}$, valid for a real Hamiltonian.
  \item Operations that do not map the \emph{mesh} onto itself are discarded.
        A $4\times4\times2$ mesh does not have the symmetry of its own
        crystal, and folding with an operation that carries a mesh point off
        the mesh would silently mis-weight the sum.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
Each irreducible point carries $w_{\vb{k}} = n_{\vb{k}}/(N_1N_2N_3)$ with
$n_{\vb{k}}$ its orbit size, and $\sum w = 1$ exactly.

\paragraph{Two reductions, and only one of them is exact.}
Folding the $k$-set changes the \emph{density}, not only the band energy.
Summing $w_{\vb{k}}\abs{\psi_{\vb{k}}}^2$ over a wedge does not reproduce the
full-zone sum, because the orbit members contribute
$\abs{\psi_{\vb{k}}(\mathcal{R}^{-1}\vb{r})}^2$ and not
$\abs{\psi_{\vb{k}}(\vb{r})}^2$; point-group folding is exact only if the
density is symmetrised afterwards, which is not yet implemented.

Time reversal is the exception, and it is exact as it stands:
$\abs{\psi_{-\vb{k}}}^2 = \abs{\psi_{\vb{k}}^{*}}^2 =
\abs{\psi_{\vb{k}}}^2$ \emph{pointwise} for a real Hamiltonian, so no
symmetrisation is needed. It is therefore the default. Measured on silicon,
everything else held fixed:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
Folding & Points ($4\times4\times4$) & $\Delta E$ vs.\ full mesh \\
\midrule
none                      & 64 & --- \\
time reversal             & 36 & $-1.1\times10^{-5}$~eV \\
crystal point group       &  8 & $+8.7\times10^{-2}$~eV \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
The point-group column is left available rather than removed, because it is
exact for \emph{eigenvalues} --- it is the right setting for a band structure
evaluated on an already-converged density --- and the engine says so at run
time rather than leaving the distinction to this document.

\paragraph{Verification.} Group orders and irreducible-wedge sizes are
published, checkable numbers, and they are checked: 48 operations for the
face-centred cubic lattice and for diamond on it, falling to 8 when one atom
is displaced; $4\times4\times4$ meshes reducing to 8, 8, 10 and 18 points for
diamond, face-centred cubic, simple cubic and the displaced case. These agreed
with an independent symmetry analyser, and they caught a real defect: building
the lattice-vector images as \emph{rows} instead of columns yields the
transposed group --- the same size, still a group, and completely invisible
for a crystal with one atom at the origin, where every operation maps the
single position onto itself regardless. Both single-atom test cases passed
while diamond was wrong.

\section{Self-consistency}
\label{sec:scf}
\implemented

The Kohn--Sham problem is a fixed point: a density gives a potential, the
potential gives orbitals, the orbitals give a density. One cycle is
\begin{equation}
  \rho \;\to\; v_{\text{es}}[\rho] + v_{xc}[\rho]
     \;\to\; H(\vb{k}), S(\vb{k})
     \;\to\; \{\varepsilon_{n\vb{k}}, C_{n\vb{k}}\}
     \;\to\; f_{n\vb{k}}
     \;\to\; \rho^{\text{out}} .
\end{equation}
The starting density is the superposition of free-atom densities, which is a
good guess: most of the density of a solid \emph{is} its atoms, and the
difference is what the SCF has to find.

\subsection{Mixing}

Feeding the output straight back diverges for anything metallic --- long
wavelength charge sloshes between regions with a gain above one --- so each
iteration is fed a mixture. Linear mixing is
$\rho^{k+1}_{\text{in}} = \rho^k_{\text{in}}
 + \alpha(\rho^k_{\text{out}} - \rho^k_{\text{in}})$.
Pulay/DIIS~\cite{pulay} treats the residual
$R^k = \rho^k_{\text{out}} - \rho^k_{\text{in}}$ as an error vector and
chooses the combination of the last $m$ inputs whose residuals cancel best,
\begin{equation}
  \min_{\{c_i\}} \norm{\sum_i c_i R^i}^2
  \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_i c_i = 1,
\end{equation}
solved as the bordered linear system
$\begin{psmallmatrix} B & \mathbf{1} \\ \mathbf{1}^{\!\top} & 0
\end{psmallmatrix}
\begin{psmallmatrix} c \\ \lambda \end{psmallmatrix} =
\begin{psmallmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{psmallmatrix}$
with $B_{ij} = R^i\cdot R^j$. The $B$ matrix becomes singular \emph{exactly}
at convergence, when the residuals are all nearly zero and therefore nearly
parallel; the implementation detects that and falls back to a linear step
rather than throwing away a converged answer on the last iteration.

\subsection{Convergence criteria and the total energy}

Both the energy change and the integrated density residual
$\int\abs{\rho^{\text{out}} - \rho^{\text{in}}}\dd\vb{r}$ must fall below
their thresholds. Requiring both is deliberate: the energy is variational and
therefore second order in the density error, so it settles while the density
is still moving. A loop that stops on energy alone stops early and reports a
density nobody converged.

The total energy is assembled as
\begin{equation}
  E \;=\; \underbrace{\sum_{n\vb{k}} f_{n\vb{k}}\varepsilon_{n\vb{k}}
          - \int \rho\, v_{\text{eff}}}_{T_s}
       \;+\; E_{\text{es}} \;+\; E_{xc}.
\end{equation}
During the loop the electrostatic and exchange--correlation terms are
evaluated on the \emph{input} density (the Harris--Foulkes form), because
building $v_{\text{es}}$ is the most expensive step in an iteration; the exact
Kohn--Sham functional is evaluated once on the converged density at the end.

\section{Energy derivatives}
\label{sec:derivatives}

\subsection{Forces}
\label{sec:forces}
\implemented

Because NAOs are \emph{atom-centred and move with the nuclei}, the force has
two parts and omitting the second produces forces inconsistent with the energy
surface. The \textbf{Hellmann--Feynman} term is the electrostatic force on the
bare nucleus,
\begin{equation}
  \vb{F}_A^{\text{HF}} = Z_A \int \rho(\vb{r})
      \frac{\vb{r}-\vb{R}_A}{\abs{\vb{r}-\vb{R}_A}^3}\dd\vb{r}
      - Z_A \sum_{B \neq A} Z_B
        \frac{\vb{R}_A-\vb{R}_B}{\abs{\vb{R}_A-\vb{R}_B}^3},
\end{equation}
and the \textbf{Pulay} term arises because
$\partial\phi_i/\partial\vb{R}_A \neq 0$:
\begin{equation}
  \vb{F}_A^{\text{Pulay}} = -\sum_{ij}
    \left[ D_{ij}\pdv{H_{ij}}{\vb{R}_A}
         - W_{ij}\pdv{S_{ij}}{\vb{R}_A} \right],
\end{equation}
with $W_{ij} = \sum f\varepsilon\,C^{*}_{i}C_{j}$ the energy-weighted density
matrix.

\paragraph{What is implemented, and what actually drives a relaxation.}
The production force is the \textbf{finite difference} $-\dd E/\dd\vb{R}_A$,
which is exact for any functional and either boundary condition because it
differences the same total energy the SCF minimises. It costs $6N$
self-consistency cycles and it is what the optimiser uses. Of the analytic
decomposition, the Hellmann--Feynman term and the \emph{overlap} part of Pulay
are implemented; $\sum_{ij}D_{ij}\partial H_{ij}/\partial\vb{R}_A$ and the
derivatives of the Becke weights are not, and on a stretched H\textsubscript{2}
bond the implemented terms give $5.2$~eV/\AA{} where the true force is $0.79$
--- the missing terms dominate. Both are reported so the gap is visible rather
than assumed away.

\paragraph{A finite-difference force is only as smooth as the energy.}
This nearly sank the whole section, and the diagnosis is worth recording. The
force initially carried between $1$ and $100$~eV/\AA{} of noise, with no
plateau in the displacement and no improvement on grid refinement. It was not
the force: the \emph{energy surface} was not converged. The neutral-atom pair
energy $\int\rho_A v_B^{\text{NA}}$ was being evaluated on atom $A$'s spherical
grid, and $v_B^{\text{NA}}$ carries the nuclear pole of $B$ --- a singularity
sitting off-centre, exactly what the multicentre partition exists to prevent.
It is invisible for a single atom, which has no pairs at all and reproduced the
radial solver to $0.001$~eV, and fatal for two.

The cure is to do that integral exactly. Both factors are spherical about
their own centres, so the angular average of $v_B$ over a sphere of radius $r$
about $A$, the centres $d$ apart, has a closed form and the pole cancels
analytically:
\begin{equation}
  \langle v_B\rangle(r) = \frac{1}{2rd}\int_{\abs{r-d}}^{r+d} v_B(t)\,t\dd t,
  \qquad
  \int\!\rho_A v_B = \frac{2\pi}{d}\int \rho_A(r)\,r
     \bigl[C(r{+}d) - C(\abs{r{-}d})\bigr]\dd r ,
\end{equation}
with $C(t) = \int_0^t v_B t'\dd t'$ and
$v_B(t)\,t = \text{screened}(t)\,t - Z_B$ smooth through the origin. This is a
one-dimensional integral on the radial mesh with no three-dimensional grid
involved. The angular drift of E(H\textsubscript{2}) fell from $0.105$~eV to
$0.0007$~eV, and the force went from noise to a clean plateau.

\paragraph{Guard.} `finiteDifference` evaluates at $h$ and $2h$ and returns a
numerical failure when they disagree. An exact derivative barely moves; grid
noise halves. That guard is what caught the pole, and it stays.

\subsection{Stress tensor}
\label{sec:stress}
\implemented

From the response of the total energy to a uniform strain, $\vb{x} \to
(\mathbb{1}+\bm{\varepsilon})\vb{x}$ applied to the lattice vectors
\emph{and} the atomic positions:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_{\alpha\beta} = \frac{1}{\Omega}\pdv{E}{\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta}} .
\end{equation}
Six independent components by central differences. Every term of the energy
contributes automatically --- the matrices through the strained inter-atomic
vectors, the electrostatics through the lattice sum and the multipole
expansion, the exchange--correlation through the volume element, and the basis
through the same mechanism that produces the Pulay force --- because all of
them sit inside the energy being differenced. An off-diagonal component
perturbs $\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta}$ and $\varepsilon_{\beta\alpha}$ together,
so its derivative carries a factor of two that is divided back out.

\paragraph{Verification.} Cubic symmetry is the sharpest available check and
needs no reference number, because nothing in the calculation is told the
crystal is cubic: for silicon the off-diagonal components come out at
$5\times10^{-10}$~eV/\AA$^3$ and the three diagonal components agree to
$1.6\times10^{-9}$. The magnitude is checked independently against a volume
scan --- the trace gives $55.9$~GPa against $52.5$~GPa from $-\dd E/\dd V$,
agreeing inside the stress's own noise estimate.

\paragraph{A strained cell is harder to converge than the one it came from.}
Coarse stress settings failed on self-consistency iterations rather than on
grid quality; gentler mixing and a higher ceiling fixed it outright. Worth
knowing before blaming the strain derivative.

\subsection{Geometry optimisation}
\implemented

Relaxation uses FIRE --- damped dynamics that removes the damping while the
system descends and freezes the velocity the moment it starts to climb. It is
chosen over a quasi-Newton method because it needs no line search: a single
force evaluation costs $6N$ self-consistency cycles, and a rejected
line-search trial would throw all of them away.

H\textsubscript{2} started at $0.95$~\AA{} relaxes monotonically to
$0.87$~\AA{} with the largest force falling from $1.76$ to $0.043$~eV/\AA{} in
twenty steps. The residual difference from the LDA reference bond length of
$0.77$~\AA{} is the confined minimal basis, not the force.

reproduces the radial answer to $0.08$~eV, and does so \emph{from above} ---
the confined basis is a restriction of the variational space, so its minimum
cannot lie below the exact one. A 3D energy underneath the 1D answer would
mean the quadrature is manufacturing binding.

\paragraph{Measured effect of the confinement barrier.}
Section~\ref{sec:confinement} argued that a hard wall leaves an unrepresented
surface term in every inter-atomic kinetic matrix element. The cohesive energy
of silicon, everything else held fixed, shows it:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
Basis & Hard wall & Smooth barrier \\
\midrule
Tier 1 (minimal)      & 7.11 & \textbf{5.68} \\
Tier 2 (DZ + polarisation) & 10.77 & \textbf{6.26} \\
Tier 3                & 66.56 & \textbf{6.41} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\par\smallskip
{\footnotesize eV/atom, $2\times2\times2$ mesh. Converged LDA is
$\approx 5.3$~eV/atom; experiment is $4.63$.}
\label{tab:wall}
\end{center}

\noindent
The wall column diverges as the basis grows --- each added function brings its
own cutoff sphere and its own missing surface term. The barrier column is a
proper convergence sequence, with increments of $+0.57$ then $+0.15$~eV. What
remains above the reference is accounted for by the spherical,
non-spin-polarised atomic reference (worth roughly $0.6$~eV for silicon),
residual confinement, and a $k$-mesh that is not yet converged
(Section~\ref{sec:ibz}).

\paragraph{What is not validated.} The absolute total energy of a
\emph{periodic} system has no external reference available: a pseudopotential
code's total energy is not comparable with an all-electron one. The periodic
path is therefore checked on what can be checked --- electron count,
self-consistency, degeneracies required by symmetry, translational invariance
--- and the absolute energy is reported without being asserted.
